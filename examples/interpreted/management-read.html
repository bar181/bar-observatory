Plate 02 · What the numbers mean

Two figures that get quoted out of context

Both of them look worse in a summary than they are in the record. Here is the arithmetic rather than the reassurance.

"Sixty errors" overstates the risk by roughly half

An error count counts tool calls that came back unhappy. It bundles two very different things: a command that failed, and a command that was stopped. In this window, 17 of the 60 were stopped by the safety system — blocked deletions and credential scans — and another 10 were never this session's failures at all.

Source 60 error rows in session.report.json → failures, grouped by the method published in the engineering companion. Read it as 33 of 60 describe something that actually went wrong, and none of those 33 is a logic defect in shipped code.

"Twenty-nine failing tests" is what iteration looks like

373 test result lines were recorded over two weeks of active development. 344 passed. A test that failed on Tuesday and passed on Thursday appears in both columns, so the total is a record of the work, not a count of broken things.

344 passed
29 failed
Last captured run · pass

Note squares are grouped by outcome, not sequenced: the store records how many lines passed and failed, not the order they arrived in. The figure that answers "is it working now" is the last captured line, and it was a pass.

Three commits for every five instructions

68 human prompts produced 41 real commits, captured from actual git commit output rather than self-reported. That ratio is the one people rarely quote and probably should.

Far fewer commits than prompts suggests work that stalls before it lands. Far more suggests changes going in without a pause for review. Three-to-five sits in the middle, and matches what the session record shows: iterate, check, commit.

It is also the number most likely to move if delivery pressure changes, which makes it worth carrying forward as a baseline rather than reading once.

482 repeat edits across 96 files. The distribution is the finding, not the total.

Why the gap is worth a question, not an alarm

77 against 22 is a five-fold gap, and it accumulated across days and separate sessions rather than inside one sitting. That is the shape of a document converging through repeated independent passes, which is a different animal from one session spent fighting a file.

The problem is not the pattern. It is that nothing in the record says which of the two it was, so the next reader has to guess. That is decision one.

What kind of work this fortnight was

Grouping every revision by file type, documentation and web pages account for 66 percent of the edits and code for 16 percent. If you were expecting two weeks of feature work, this is the number that says otherwise.

Plate 04 · Who did the work

More than a third of delegated work was checking

What that ratio buys you

A quarter of every delegation went to an independent reviewer role alone, and 37.5 percent went to some form of checking. On a two-week push where no human read every line, that ratio is the strongest quality signal in the window — and a stronger one than the error count implies to the contrary.

Plate 05 · Your decisions

Two things only you can close

Neither is a fault. Both are open because the record can describe the situation but cannot choose what should happen next.

  1. Decide whether the 77-edit review pass is standard practice, and write it down

    Those edits map to a known multi-reviewer pass — separate passes for a first-time reader, an executive reader, a fact-checker, and search visibility, each finding real problems. Read that way it is diligence, and the output is better for it. The risk is that nothing records that. The next file that shows this pattern without a documented process behind it will look identical to instability, and there will be no way to tell from the numbers which one it was.

    Process · one paragraph in the contributing guide closes it

  2. Decide whether sessions like these need to be costed

    Every money and token figure in this window reads "not recorded". That is deliberate wording: the tooling refuses to print a zero it did not measure, because a zero would read as free rather than unmeasured. These sessions ran on a flat subscription, so no per-request price existed to capture. Getting a cost figure means enabling a separate capture channel before the next block of work — a one-time setup, and nothing recovers the fortnight already past.

    Budget · setup decision, not a data gap that self-heals

What "commentary" means here

A model was given a fixed brief of facts it was not allowed to change, and wrote this page from it. Switch the model off and the evidence underneath is untouched. The deterministic delivery memo is the same window with no prose at all.

Where to push back

Dispute the readings, not the counts. If you think the 77-edit pattern means something other than a review pass, that is a live disagreement — and it is settled by looking at the file's history, which this page does not claim to have done.

Two dates, and why they differ

The scope window recorded in the store runs 2026-05-04T19:07Z to 2026-05-18T06:06Z UTC. The seven sessions are labelled by the day each one started, so the last reads 2026-05-17 while the window closes on the 18th. Both are stated rather than silently reconciled; the elapsed span is 322h 59m either way.
